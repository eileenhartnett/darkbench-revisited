What a Benchmark Replication Reveals About LLM Evaluation

Lessons on judge reliability, measurement validity, and evaluating model behavior

Eileen Hartnett

Abstract

I re-ran DarkBench, a benchmark published at ICLR 2025 that uses 660 prompts to try to make a chatbot manipulate the user, on nine current models and three models from 2024, with three current LLM judges. The original tested models from 2024 and scored them with judges from 2024. Rates are lower than the figure usually quoted from the paper, about 20% against 48%. That comparison does not carry the weight it is given. The 48% is one annotator's panel (GPT-4o); the paper's other two annotators average 32% and 43% on the same responses. The 2024 models do score lower in my pipeline, but I generated fresh responses rather than re-scoring the paper's, so the judge effect is not isolated. The clearest behavioural change is sycophancy, as DarkBench defines it, now near the floor for most current models on this prompt set, with one exception.

Along the way the study measured things benchmark papers usually do not: how much a judge disagrees with itself (little, κ 0.87 to 0.96), how much judges disagree with each other (a lot, κ 0.52 to 0.57), and whether a human agrees with any of them (partly, and it depends on the category).

None of this diminishes DarkBench. It was an early, careful attempt to measure something that matters, and its authors validated their judges against human labels when few did. What has changed since is the science of evaluation itself: the field now knows more about what sways a judged score and what weakens construct validity. This paper turns the replication into eight lessons about those sources of variance and the research-design choices that account for them. The DarkBench numbers are the worked example inside each lesson, not the target. None of the individual lessons is new; each is already established somewhere in the evaluation literature. What this adds is working through all of them on a single benchmark that people are still citing and still running, with real numbers attached to what each one turns up.

TakeawayBefore trusting an evaluation that uses LLM judges, test each judge against itself, not only against other judges. Moderate agreement between judges looks the same whether the judges are unreliable or the categories are vague. Only a self-consistency check tells the two apart.

Each lesson has the same four parts: the idea, what happened here, what to look out for, and what I did and did not do about it. The checklist near the end is the part to print. Numbers carry a pointer in brackets to the section of the full writeup (W), the supplement (S), or the CSV they come from. Appendix A is the full methods; Appendix B holds every table.
